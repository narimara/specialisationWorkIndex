Synopsis

Arcade Synth is a browser based synthesiser instrument designed to be played by two people sharing one computer. It aims to provide users the opportunity to spent time and bond over a creative endeavor, and is largely an explorative project that proposes music creation as a simplified, casual and cooperative experience. Two users split controls of the instrument between mouse and keyboard, with mouse handling note creation, waveform, volume, speed, and pause and play, and keyboard handling the timing and pitch of each created note.

Design Scenario

My chosen design scenario was the multiplayer synthesiser instrument. The brief highlights that music is often played in collaboration with others but browser-based spaces for this are quite lacking. To address this, a key defining feature must be the ability for multiple people to use the instrument on one computer. To me, this sounded like the core principles should be encouraging casual collaboration, allowing people to bond with each other through a creative outlet. The purpose serves both in making music and bringing people closer together. This could be interpreted through multiple means, but the one that stuck out to me the most was essentially gamifying the process of making music, to make it more fun to do with other people.

Response

My response was driven by this interpretation of the design scenario, and I leaned heavily into gamification. I borrowed from the structure of rhythm games and ensured the instrument relied on both users to work in collaboration rather than independently making noise. I did this through the controls between mouse and keyboard, and locking some elements of interaction that force players to build off each other and communicate.

Arcade Synth

Core Interactive Features

Mouse and Keyboard Split Controls

Splitting controls between mouse and keyboard shaped all other interactions. The user on mouse controls global parameters like waveform, playback speed, volume and note spawning while the user on keyboard uses predominantly the arrow keys to arrange the notes into music.

Spatial Input

The pitch and timing of notes is decided by a 12 x 16 grid which acts as a stave. The position of notes on this grid along the x axis determines their timing in 12 looping beats, while the y axis determines pitch on the chromatic scale.

Randomised Note Length

Notes created appear on the stave at a randomly selected length of between 1 and 4 beats as an extra challenge for creativity through a simple constant that selects a number from 1 to 4 every time the add note button is pressed.

Looping Playback

When play is enabled, a visible playhead scans from left to right over the stage, animated using javascript, and plays notes according to their stave position as it crosses them.

Extended Technique

Spatial Input

As I briefly described in the previous section, the spatial input in Arcade Synth works through a grid that locks notes to increments of 12 and 16 along its axes. I call this section of the display a stave because it is intended to transform traditional music notation into a simpler and more intuitive system. The grid does not operate on freeform spatial coordinates as this made it difficult to tune pitch in a way that sounded pleasant. Instead, it functions on a step integer system which locks note objects to increments of a scale of 1-16 vertically and 1-12 horizontally. Audio feedback is processed through note collision detection of a vertical playhead animated to sweep through the stave area from left to right.

Design System

I created a moodboard with a visual style inspired by retro arcade hardware. It felt fitting to my game-inspired concept to keep the visual style similarly game-like (hence the word arcade in the name of the instrument) and I liked the idea of glowing elements against a dark background. I used a dark canvas with a high contrast palette of cyan and green, using the accent colours for highlighting interactive elements with borders and state changes on hover or selection. Buttons also use greyscale filtering and reduced opacity when disabled to communicate interaction locks.

My chosen fonts are Share Tech Mono for labels and buttons, and Space Grotesk for other visible text. Both fonts are licensed under the open font license for use in products and projects, print or digital, commercial or otherwise.

My spatial layout of all the elements, as well as the design of buttons, sliders, and instructions was determined through user testing. The result keeps the interface clean and easy to navigate, with only few interactive elements that can be played with and arranged in endless combinations.
